Workout Spreadsheet vs App: Which Tracks Training Better?

Updated July 2026 · by the Monolift team

Lifting runs on spreadsheets. 5/3/1, nSuns, GZCLP — the canonical versions of these programs are literally Google Sheets, passed around Reddit and hosted on sites like Lift Vault. So the workout spreadsheet vs app question is not about stubborn lifters refusing progress. Spreadsheet people keep their sheets because sheets do real things apps still can't. The honest comparison comes down to the one thing spreadsheets do badly — and it happens twenty times every session.

Short answer: keep the spreadsheet for program design if the math matters to you — nothing beats a sheet for percentage-based programming and custom analysis. But move the day-to-day logging somewhere you can enter a set one-handed in a couple of seconds, because that is the part a spreadsheet on a phone is genuinely bad at. Most lifters who abandon their sheet never quit tracking — they quit editing cells between sets.

Why the workout log spreadsheet earned its place

Worth conceding up front: for programming, a spreadsheet is the best tool anyone has built.

  • Program math. Enter a training max and a 5/3/1 sheet calculates every set of every week of the cycle. Percentage waves, AMRAP-driven progression, deload weeks — formulas handle all of it, for any program you can describe.
  • Custom views. Estimated 1RM trend next to weekly tonnage next to per-lift volume? Build it. An app shows the charts its developer chose. A sheet shows anything you can express in a formula.
  • Data ownership. It's a file. Copy it, back it up, open it in ten years. No account required and no company that can shut down.
  • Cost. Google Sheets is free and most program templates are free downloads.

If an app can't beat the sheet at those four things — and mostly it can't — it has to win somewhere else.

Tracking workouts in a spreadsheet: where it breaks down

It breaks down in the gym, on your phone, between sets.

Logging a set in mobile Sheets goes like this: wake the phone, open the app, wait for it to load, pinch-zoom to your row, tap a cell small enough that you hit the one next to it, bring up the keyboard, type the number, tap the next cell, repeat. That's fifteen to thirty seconds per set, two hands, and one fat-thumbed tap away from overwriting the formula that drives your whole cycle.

Then the smaller frictions stack. There's no rest timer, so you juggle a second app. Checking what you lifted last week means scrolling the grid mid-session. Charts exist only if you built them and stay correct only if you maintain them. Nothing tells you that last set was a rep PR — you find out later, if you go looking.

So most spreadsheet lifters do the natural thing: stop logging between sets and backfill the sheet from memory after training. That's where data quality quietly dies. Tracking progressive overload only works when the numbers are what actually happened, not what you reconstructed in the car.

When you should keep the sheet

Some lifters genuinely shouldn't switch. Keep the spreadsheet as your logbook if:

  • You run heavily percentage-based programming and the formulas are doing real work every week.
  • You coach other people — a shared sheet is still the simplest way to program for a handful of clients.
  • You're in meet prep with planned attempts and you want the plan and the log in one place.
  • You actually open the data and analyze it. If you use the pivot tables, you've earned the friction.

There's also a hybrid that a lot of experienced lifters land on, and it's the honest recommendation of this page: the sheet stays as the program-design document and the long-term archive. Only the logging moves.

When an app is the better tool

If your sheet is really just a grid of weights and reps — no formulas doing heavy lifting — you're paying spreadsheet friction for spreadsheet power you don't use. An app gives you the rest timer, the history at a glance, the charts, and the PR detection without maintenance.

The well-known structured trackers are good at this. Hevy and Strong both give you an exercise database, tidy history, and solid charts, and both can export your data to CSV (as of mid-2026), so the ownership argument against apps is weaker than it used to be — we compare them directly in Hevy vs Strong. Their trade-off is that logging is form-filling: pick the exercise from a list, tap into the weight field, tap into the reps field, save. It's better than a sheet on a phone, but it's still a form. Some lifters try a structured tracker, feel slower than they did with their grid, and go back to the sheet. That instinct is correct — the grid was fast to type into on a keyboard. The problem was never the grid. It was the phone.

A third option: a log that's still plain text

Monolift starts from the reason the spreadsheet existed: typing into a grid was fast, and the data stayed readable. So the log is a plain-text note. You type a workout the way you'd type it in the sheet's notes column:

Bench Press: 185 lbs 5, 5, 5
Incline DB Press: 70 lbs 10, 10, 8
Lat Pulldown: 130 lbs 12, 12, 10

Colon after the exercise, commas between sets, and it live-renders into cards with tappable weight and rep pills as you type. To change a number you don't touch the keyboard at all — long-press it and slide. That's drag-to-dial: about two seconds a set, one thumb. A plate mode steps through real plate loads with a haptic tick, which beats typing 172.5 into a cell every time.

In Monolift Start a workout and type Bench Press: 185 lbs 5, 5, 5 — it becomes a card as you type. Next session, long-press last week's 185 and slide it to 190; you never retype the exercise. One tap adds a set, and the separators are customizable if your sheet used different notation. Rest timers run as Lock Screen Live Activities, so there's no second app to juggle.

The part the sheet made you build by hand comes free. Every set you log feeds a per-exercise progress chart, and weight and rep PRs are detected automatically — the app tells you when a set is a PR instead of a formula you wrote two years ago and hope still works.

In Monolift Open any exercise to see its chart and PR history — no setup. If part of your history lives on paper, photo import (a Pro feature, included in the free 7-day trial) reads a page of your log into the app. The AI coach (also Pro) reads your actual training history and can flag a plateau or suggest a deload — see when to deload for how that works.

What Monolift doesn't do, so you're not surprised: it won't calculate your 5/3/1 cycle for you — there's no percentage engine, which is exactly why the hybrid setup works. Design the block in your sheet, paste each week into the log as plain text. Switching without losing your system

Migration is smaller than it sounds because there's nothing to convert. Your sheet stays where it is, as the archive and the design doc. Moving the log means typing or pasting next week's plan into a note — exercises, colons, weights, commas — which takes a few minutes, not an afternoon. There's no automatic import from other apps; plain text is the import format.

If your log is a paper notebook rather than a sheet, the same argument runs even stronger — that comparison is in gym notebook vs app. If you want to test plain-text logging before installing anything, our Apple Notes workout template is the free version of the same idea. And if you're rethinking what's worth recording at all, start with how to track workouts.

If your spreadsheet's real job is holding the numbers between sessions, type your next workout as plain text and let the charts and PRs build themselves.
