How to Log Your Workouts: What to Track and the Fastest Way to Do It

Updated July 2026 · by the Monolift team

Learning how to log workouts is mostly about avoiding one mistake: building a system so thorough that you quit using it by week three. Lifters who have years of clean training data almost all record the same three things and ignore everything else. Here is what to write down, an honest comparison of the four ways to do it, and why the fastest system usually ends up being the one that lasts.

The short answer — for every working set, log:

  • the exercise name
  • the weight you used
  • the reps you actually got, set by set (not a range)
  • optional: RPE and a one-line note when something felt off

Then put it in the fastest tool you will actually open. A plain note you keep beats a perfect spreadsheet you abandon.

Should you track your workouts at all?

Honest answer: not everyone needs to. If you train for general health, enjoy showing up, and are not chasing specific strength or muscle goals, a log adds admin without much payoff. Skip it with a clear conscience.

If you want to get measurably stronger or bigger, the answer flips to yes. Progress in the gym comes from progressive overload — doing slightly more than last time, week after week. That only works if you know what "last time" was. Memory handles this for about two weeks; once you rotate through a few different sessions, "what did I row last Tuesday?" becomes a guess, and guesses drift downward. The log is simply the reference number that makes your next session count.

There is a second, quieter benefit: when progress stalls, a log tells you whether you are actually stuck or just impatient. Three weeks at the same bench number in the log is a real plateau you can act on. A vague feeling that "bench isn't moving" is not.

What should you write in a workout log?

Three things per working set: exercise, weight, reps. The per-set rep counts matter more than people think — the difference between 8, 8, 8 and 8, 7, 5 at the same weight is the difference between "add weight next time" and "stay here another week." A weekly total or a rep range hides exactly the signal you are logging for.

A complete session log can be this small:

Bench Press: 135 lbs 8, 8, 6
Incline DB Press: 50 lbs 10, 10, 9
Cable Row: 120 lbs 12, 12, 12
felt the bench slow on set 3, shoulder fine

Two optional extras earn their place. RPE — a 1-to-10 rating of how hard the set felt — is worth adding once you are past the beginner stage, because 135 for 8 at RPE 7 and 135 for 8 at RPE 9.5 call for different next steps. And a short note ("low sleep," "tweaked wrist," "paused reps") gives future-you context that numbers alone miss.

Just as important is what to leave out. Warm-up sets, rest times to the second, session duration, mood scores, machine seat settings for every station — most of it never gets read again. Every extra field you commit to logging is friction, and friction is what kills logs. Body weight and food are worth tracking for a lot of lifters, but they belong in their own place, not stuffed into the workout entry.

The best way to track workouts: four methods compared

There are four realistic options, and each one trades speed against structure. None of them is wrong; they fail in different ways.

Apple NotesFastManual scrolling and searchNo charts, no PRs, freeform mess over time
SpreadsheetSlow on a phoneExcellent, if you build itMid-set data entry; upkeep becomes a hobby
Dedicated appVaries — some are tap-heavyAutomatic charts and PRsBloat, subscriptions, rigid workout structure

Paper is underrated. A notebook never runs an update mid-set and never asks you to pick from a dropdown. Its problem is that the data goes in and never comes back out — comparing this month's squat to March means flipping pages. If you are weighing the two, our gym notebook vs app breakdown goes deeper.

Apple Notes is where a huge number of lifters land, for good reason: it is already on your phone and typing "Bench 135: 8, 8, 6" takes five seconds. We wrote a full Apple Notes workout template if you want to run this system properly. Its ceiling is real, though — Notes never turns your history into a chart, never spots a PR, and after six months you are scrolling a wall of text to find one number.

Spreadsheets give you the best analysis of any method — e1RM formulas, volume charts, whatever you can build. The cost is entering data into cells on a phone with a barbell in front of you, which is why most spreadsheet logs get filled in from memory hours later, or not at all. Dedicated apps automate the analysis: charts, PR detection, rest timers, history at your thumb. Hevy and Strong are the two big names, and both are genuinely good — large exercise libraries, automatic rest timers, solid progress charts, and in Hevy's case a social feed your gym friends may already be on. The common complaint is structure: most apps make you build routines, pick exercises from a database, and tap through fields for every set, which is slower than scribbling a line — and it is the main reason people go looking for Hevy alternatives or Strong alternatives after a few months. How to track gym progress once you have the data

A log you never read is a diary. To track gym progress, you need three reads on the data:

Last session's numbers, mid-workout. This is the one you use every single session: what did I lift last time, so I know what to attempt today. Any method that makes this slow — page-flipping, scrolling, opening a laptop — fails at the log's main job.

PRs. Rep PRs and weight PRs are the milestones that tell you the plan is working. On paper or in Notes you have to notice them yourself, and most people miss their own rep PRs entirely.

The trend line. Per-exercise charts over months are how you distinguish steady progress from a genuine stall. When a lift has been flat for weeks despite honest effort, that is usually fatigue, not weakness — our guide on when to deload covers reading that signal and acting on it, and the progressive overload guide covers what to do when the trend is healthy.
