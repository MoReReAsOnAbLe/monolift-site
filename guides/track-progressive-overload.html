How to Track Progressive Overload: PRs, Charts, and the 2-for-2 Rule

Updated July 2026 · by the Monolift team

You don't need a coach standing over you to know whether you're getting stronger. You need last session's numbers. Knowing how to track progressive overload comes down to three habits: write down every working set, compare it against the last time you did that exercise, and judge the trend over weeks — not single workouts.

The short answer: log the weight and reps for every working set, then try to beat one of those numbers next session. Add weight using the 2-for-2 rule — when you get two reps over your target on the last set, in two consecutive sessions, increase the load (roughly 2.5–5 lb on upper-body lifts, 5–10 lb on lower-body lifts). Judge real progress over 4–8 week windows using three PR types: weight PRs, rep PRs, and estimated-1RM PRs.

That's the whole system. The rest of this guide unpacks each piece: the rule for when to add weight, the PRs actually worth counting, and what "progressing" looks like when you zoom out past a single Tuesday.

How often should you increase weight lifting? Use the 2-for-2 rule

Not on a schedule. The useful answer to "how often should I add weight" is a performance trigger, not a calendar date — and the standard trigger is the 2-for-2 rule.

The 2-for-2 rule: if you can do two reps more than your target on the last set of an exercise, in two consecutive sessions, add weight the next time you train that exercise.

Example. Your program calls for bench press, 3 sets of 8 at 185 lb. Last week your final set was 10 reps. Today it was 10 again. Two extra reps, two sessions running — next bench day you load 190.

Standard increments are 2.5–5 lb for upper-body lifts and 5–10 lb for lower-body lifts, roughly 2–5% of the load. Smaller is usually better. A 5 lb jump on an overhead press is a bigger relative jump than it feels, and microplates exist for a reason.

How often the rule fires depends on where you are. A newer lifter squatting three times a week might trigger it almost every week for months. An intermediate might go four to six weeks between increases on a press, grinding out rep PRs in between. Both are normal. The rule self-regulates: you never add weight you haven't earned, and you never sit on a weight you've outgrown.

Notice what the rule quietly assumes — you know exactly what your last set was, last session. No log, no rule.

How to track PRs in the gym: the three PR types

"PR" usually gets used to mean one thing: the heaviest weight you've ever lifted. For tracking purposes that's the least useful definition, because if you train in the 5–12 rep range you might not touch a true max for months. There are three PR types, and most of your progress shows up in the other two.

PR typeWhat it isExampleWhat it tells you
Weight PRThe heaviest you've ever lifted on that exercise230 lb × 1 after a previous best of 225Top-end strength. Rare by design if you train higher reps.
Rep PRThe most reps you've ever done at a given weight185 lb × 9 after a best of 185 × 8The workhorse. This is the PR the 2-for-2 rule runs on.
Estimated-1RM PRYour best weight-times-reps set, converted to a one-rep max with a formula185 × 8 (≈234 lb estimated) beats 200 × 3 (≈220)The trend line. Lets sets from different rep ranges compare fairly.

The common conversion is the Epley formula: weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30). It's an estimate, not a prophecy — it gets loose above about 10 reps — but it's consistent, and consistent is all a trend line needs to be.

If you only track one number per exercise, make it estimated 1RM. It catches progress a max-weight view misses entirely: 185 for 8 is a stronger set than 200 for 3, even though the bar was lighter.

How to know if you're progressing in the gym

Single sessions lie. Bad sleep, a stressful week, training at 6 a.m. instead of 6 p.m. — any of these can knock a few percent off your lifts for a day. So don't ask "was today better than last Tuesday?" Ask "was this month better than last month?"

Over a 4–8 week window, you're progressing if any of these are true on your main lifts:

  • Estimated 1RM is trending up. Even 2–3% across a month is real progress for anyone past the beginner stage.
  • You're setting rep PRs at the same weights. Same bar, more reps is progressive overload, full stop.
  • The same weight is moving better. A set that was a grinder six weeks ago is now clean. Effort dropping at a fixed load counts, even before the numbers move.
  • You're doing more total work at the same quality. An extra set at the same weight, without form falling apart.

Context matters too. Holding every lift flat while dieting is progress. Judging a cut by bulk standards is how lifters talk themselves into believing they're going backwards when they're not.

And if none of these are true across 4–8 weeks of honest effort, that's not a bad month — that's a signal. More on that below.

How to track progressive overload session to session

Everything above runs on one input: a record of what you actually did. The minimum viable log is three things per exercise — name, weight, reps for every working set:

Bench Press: 185 lbs 8, 8, 10
Incline DB Press: 70 lbs 10, 9, 8
Chest-Supported Row: 155 lbs 12, 12, 11

Add a note whenever context changes — paused reps, a different bar, a new gym's machines. Future you needs to know why 155 suddenly reads like 140.

Where you keep the log matters less than whether last session's numbers are in front of you at the rack. A paper notebook genuinely works; lifters got strong for decades with nothing else. Its weakness isn't the writing, it's the retrieval: your last bench session is three pages back, February lives in a different notebook, and nobody computes estimated 1RMs by hand between sets. A spreadsheet fixes the math and the charts, but is miserable to type into mid-set on a phone. Tracker apps like Hevy and Strong solve retrieval properly — as of mid-2026 both show your previous numbers per exercise, flag PRs, and chart your history, and both are good at it. Their trade-off is the logging itself: forms, pickers, and a tap-per-field for every set, which is exactly the friction that gets logs abandoned by week three.

When the numbers stop moving

A bad session is noise. Three or more weeks with no PR of any type — no weight PR, no rep PR, no estimated-1RM PR — at honest effort is a stall.

Check the boring things first: sleep, food, and whether "honest effort" has quietly become stopping four reps short. If those check out, the usual fix is a deload — a planned easy week that lets accumulated fatigue clear so the trend can resume. Telling fatigue apart from an actual plateau is its own topic; when to deload covers the signals and how to run the week.

This is where tracking earns its keep. Without a log, a stall and a rough patch feel identical. With six weeks of numbers in front of you, you know which one you're in — and whether the fix is rest or a program change.
